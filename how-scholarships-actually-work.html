What A Scholarship Actually Means, And Why So Many People Get It Wrong

Scholarship, grant, bursary, fellowship, assistantship: people use these words like they mean the same thing. They don't, and the gap between what you assume and what's actually in the offer letter is where plans fall apart.

Cipher Educational Consult · August 2026 · 11 min read

In this guide

Most people use "scholarship" as a catch-all word for "someone else pays for my degree." That's not what it means, and it's not one thing, it's a whole family of arrangements with different rules, different obligations, and different ways of quietly falling apart if you don't understand which one you actually signed up for.

This is the long version, on purpose. Everything else we publish about individual countries and scholarships builds on what's in this one, so it's worth getting right once instead of relearning it every time.

1. The words people mix up

These five words get used interchangeably. They shouldn't be.

  • Scholarship: money awarded for merit or achievement. No repayment, no work required in exchange. This is the one people picture when they hear the word.
  • Grant: usually awarded for demonstrated financial need, or for a specific purpose like research. Also doesn't need repaying, but it's often tied to a condition, a project, a need threshold, rather than pure achievement.
  • Bursary: usually need-based, common in Nigeria and the wider Commonwealth system. State scholarship boards in Nigeria typically run a bursary department separately from their foreign scholarship department, they're treated as genuinely different things internally.
  • Fellowship: usually postgraduate or postdoctoral funding, often built around being part of a research community, not just receiving a payment.
  • Assistantship: this is the one to slow down on. A teaching or research assistantship is a job. You work 10 to 20 hours a week, teaching a class or assisting a professor's research, and in exchange you get a tuition waiver plus a stipend. It's extremely common at PhD level, and increasingly at Master's level too. There's nothing wrong with it, it's often a generous way to fund a degree, but it comes with real hours and real responsibilities. Someone who thinks they "got a scholarship" when they actually signed up for a part-time job attached to their degree is going to have a rough first semester.

2. What "fully funded" really covers

"Fully funded" usually means tuition plus some form of stipend or living allowance. Past that, it varies a lot, and the gap between what's assumed and what's actually written down is where people get caught out financially.

Commonly included: tuition, a monthly stipend, on-campus housing or a housing allowance, health insurance.

Commonly missing unless it's spelled out: flights (some scholarships cover this once, some every year, many not at all), visa fees, the gap between arriving and your first stipend payment landing, which can run four to eight weeks in some systems, and setup costs, winter clothing if you're heading somewhere cold, a local phone line, groceries before that first payment clears.

Partial scholarships usually cover tuition only, or a percentage of it. A "50% scholarship" on a $30,000-a-year program still leaves $15,000 a year in tuition, plus full living costs. That's a very different reality than the phrase "50% scholarship" sounds like on its own.

The rule that actually protects you: get the line-item breakdown in writing before you count on anything that isn't explicitly named.

3. Where the money actually comes from

Four different levels fund scholarships, and they rarely talk to each other.

  • The university itself, from its own endowment or budget. Common at well-resourced private universities, and increasingly common at ambitious universities elsewhere trying to attract international students. Individual Chinese universities, Tsinghua, Peking, Fudan, Xiamen, and others, all run scholarships of their own, separate from any national scheme.
  • The host country's national government, as a tool for attracting international talent. China's Chinese Government Scholarship, commonly called CSC after the China Scholarship Council that runs it, is the most visible current example, funding roughly 15,000 international students a year across more than 280 universities.
  • The host country's provincial or state government, a level below the national one. In China specifically, provinces including Beijing, Shanghai, Jiangsu, and Zhejiang each run their own scholarship programs, separate from CSC, and sometimes stackable with partial CSC funding.
  • Your own home government, the one people forget to check. Nigeria runs its own funding sources: the Petroleum Technology Development Fund (PTDF) offers a fully funded MSc and PhD scholarship for Nigerians in oil and gas-relevant fields, covering tuition, flights, insurance, accommodation, and a monthly stipend, at partner universities in the UK, Germany, France, and Malaysia, or domestically at the College of Petroleum and Energy Studies in Kaduna. It doesn't fund undergraduate study. State governments run their own boards too. Lagos State's Scholarship Board, for example, runs separate bursary and foreign scholarship departments, though awards are restricted to Lagos State indigenes.

4. How it changes by degree level

Bachelor's is the hardest level to fund from outside your own country. Most national scholarship schemes cap eligibility around age 25 for undergraduate applicants, competition is steepest here, and this is exactly the level where China's new academic entrance exam, covered below, now applies.

Master's opens up considerably. Age limits typically extend to around 35, and both universities and national schemes compete harder for strong Master's candidates, since they tend to be research-productive faster than undergraduates.

PhD is usually the most heavily funded level, but the logic changes entirely. Funding is frequently tied to a specific supervisor who has to actually want to take you on, it's often structured as an assistantship rather than a no-strings award, and a pre-acceptance letter from that supervisor is commonly required before you can even formally apply for national-level funding on top of it.

5. Getting it vs keeping it

Three different things decide whether you're awarded funding in the first place:

  • Merit-based: academic achievement, test scores, a strong application.
  • Need-based: demonstrated financial need, usually backed by income documentation.
  • Automatic vs competitive university waivers: an automatic waiver applies itself once you hit a stated GPA or test-score threshold, no separate application needed. A competitive merit waiver means even admitted students have to separately compete for a limited pool. A need-based waiver runs through a financial aid assessment, independent of how strong your grades are.

What almost nobody explains upfront is the part that happens after the award: most scholarships, CSC included, review your academic performance and conduct every year. Fall below the required grade average, fail a course, or get flagged for a conduct issue, and funding isn't just "not renewed", it can be actively suspended or pulled, mid-degree. That's the real condition to ask about before you accept anything, not just what gets you in.

6. The competition reality

CSC's own acceptance rate runs roughly 10% to 30%, depending on the specific university and program, tighter at the most prestigious schools, looser at mid-tier ones. Tough, but nowhere near as brutal as something like the Rhodes Scholarship, which sits under 1%.

What evaluators actually look for: a GPA around 3.3 out of 4.0 (roughly 80 out of 100) is commonly cited as the practical floor for CSC-level competitiveness, a study plan written specifically for the exact program and university you're applying to, not a generic essay that could apply anywhere, and for research-based Master's and PhD applications, an actual pre-arranged match with a supervisor before you submit anything.

Bachelor's applicants face the steepest climb specifically because, on top of everything above, they now also have to clear a new academic entrance exam that Master's and PhD applicants don't face at all.

7. How and when you actually apply

Two channels repeat across many national scholarship systems, not just China's: a government channel, where you apply through your home country's designated authority, and a direct-to-institution channel, where you apply straight to the university, which often requires securing a pre-acceptance or recommendation letter from a professor there first.

That sequencing is exactly where people lose months. For many university-direct routes, the university's pre-acceptance has to come first, then you use it to apply for the funding, not the other way around.

Timing: apply 9 to 12 months before your intended start date, not weeks. Government channels commonly open in October and close by December or January for a scholarship starting the following September. University-direct channels can run anywhere from December to March. And this catches people constantly: individual universities frequently close their own internal deadline earlier than the system-wide deadline everyone fixates on, so the date on the national portal is not necessarily the date that matters for your specific school.

8. Current example: China's new CSCA exam

Starting with the 2026/2027 intake, anyone applying to an undergraduate program at a CSC-funded Chinese university has to sit the China Scholastic Competency Assessment (CSCA), first held on December 21, 2025. It tests Mathematics for every applicant, Professional Chinese if the program is taught in Chinese, and Physics or Chemistry depending on the intended major. Master's and PhD applicants don't take it at all, it's undergraduate-only. China's stated plan is to make it mandatory for all international undergraduate applicants nationwide, not just CSC-funded ones, by 2028.

This is exactly why a guide written a year ago can be wrong for someone applying this cycle. We'll go deeper on how to actually prepare for the CSCA in the next piece.

9. The scam warning

A real scholarship application, at every level described above, does not require paying anyone to "guarantee" you a spot or "process" your application faster. You will pay for legitimate things along the way, standardized test fees, document notarization, medical exams, but nobody legitimate charges a fee for the scholarship decision itself. If someone is promising a guaranteed scholarship for a fee, that's the red flag, not the scholarship.

Get this part right first. Everything specific to a country or a scholarship, the numbers, the exams, the deadlines, sits on top of the definitions above, and it changes fast enough that "what I read last year" isn't good enough anymore.

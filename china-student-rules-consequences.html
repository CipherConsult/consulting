What Actually Happens If You Break The Rules As A Student In China

Working without permission, running a business on the side, overstaying by a few days. Here's the real cost of each, in fines, bans, and time, not vague warnings.

Cipher Educational Consult · October 2026 · 8 min read

In this guide

1. Working without permission 2. Running a business on the side 3. Overstaying, even by a few days 4. Breaking the law entirely 5. A September 2026 rule that protects you 6. The one question to ask your agent

None of this is meant to scare you off studying in China. It's meant to replace the vague "be careful over there" warning with the actual rules, the actual numbers, and what genuinely is allowed, because most of what circulates about this online is either wrong or deliberately fuzzy.

1. Working without permission

Since January 12, 2022, international students enrolled in degree-granting Chinese universities are allowed to do work-study, on or off campus, but only inside tight limits. You generally need to have studied at your university for at least a year, and hold a residence permit with at least six months left on it. Hours are capped at 8 a week and 40 a month during term, and 16 a week and 80 a month during winter and summer vacations. For anything off campus, your university has to approve it first, and the local Exit and Entry bureau has to record the employer and the period on your residence permit. Skip either step, and you're not "working part-time", you're working illegally, even if the job itself is completely ordinary.

Under Article 80 of China's Exit and Entry Administration Law, working illegally carries a fine of RMB 5,000 to 20,000. In serious cases that becomes 5 to 15 days of detention on top of the fine. You can also be ordered to leave China, or deported, which brings a re-entry ban of 1 to 5 years if you're ordered out, and 10 years if you're formally deported.

Not knowing the rule doesn't change the outcome. Individual universities and cities can apply these limits slightly differently, so confirm the approval is actually recorded on your residence permit before you start, not after.

2. Running a business on the side

You cannot legally operate a business, manage one day-to-day, or draw any income, salary, or dividend from one while you're on a student visa. That's the rule in plain terms, and it holds regardless of how the business is structured.

What you can do: prepare. Writing a business plan and researching your market are preparation. Operating the business, managing it day to day, or drawing income from it is what crosses the line. Whether you can even register a company while on a student permit depends on the local registration authority, so get the answer in writing before you try.

There is one narrow, real exception: Shanghai's Zhangjiang zone has run a pilot that lets eligible international students at partner universities start a business there while still studying. It is a local pilot, not a nationwide rule. Outside that zone, the standard path is to finish your degree first, then transition to an entrepreneurship-designated residence permit or a work visa built around the company, which several universities and city programs now support.

3. Overstaying, even by a few days

Don't assume a few days over is harmless. Under Article 78, the baseline for illegal residence is a warning. Where the circumstances are serious, the penalty is a fine, or detention, and "serious" is a judgment call made by the officer in front of you, not by you.

In serious cases the fine is RMB 500 per day, capped at RMB 10,000, or 5 to 15 days of detention. You can't leave until a fine is settled, and recent guides consistently report that it has to be paid in cash or by a local Chinese bank card, since foreign cards often fail at the counter. Deportation is also possible, with the same re-entry bans as above: 1 to 5 years if you're ordered out, 10 years if you're formally deported.

If you realise your permit is about to run out, or already has, go to the local Exit and Entry office before you try to leave, not after you're stopped at the airport. Because the law makes a warning the starting point, an early, voluntary visit is the best position to be in.

4. Breaking the law entirely

This one doesn't run through immigration law at all, it runs through China's ordinary criminal code, the same as it would for a Chinese citizen, and immigration consequences stack on top of whatever criminal penalty applies.

In practical terms: the Exit and Entry Administration Law allows a foreigner involved in a criminal case to be barred from leaving China while it's open. Your university also has its own conduct rules, which run separately from the courts and can end in expulsion. A conviction means whatever sentence China's criminal code prescribes for that offense, and deportation can follow, which carries the long re-entry bans described above. We have deliberately not put numbers on this section, because the penalties depend entirely on the offense.

5. A September 2026 rule that protects you

China's State Council signed new Exit and Entry Administration Provisions on July 22, 2026, published them on July 31, and brought them into force on September 15, 2026. Law firm reviews describe it as one of the most significant updates to this framework since the Exit and Entry Administration Law took effect in 2013. Most of it deals with exit controls on Chinese citizens, sanctions-related entry restrictions, and immigration agencies, not ordinary students, and for the average student, daily life shouldn't look any different.

One part of it is directly useful to you: any agency offering exit-entry or immigration consultation services inside China must now file with the local immigration authority. New agencies must file within 15 days of starting up, and agencies already operating have until mid-December 2026. Foreign institutions are not allowed to provide these intermediary services inside China at all. For the first time, there's a checkable standard for "is the agency handling this in China legitimate."

Every consequence above has one thing in common: almost nobody ends up in this position because they meant to break a rule. It's because someone guiding them didn't know the rule, or didn't say.

6. The one question to ask your agent

"Are you registered, and can I see it?" The new Chinese filing rule covers agencies operating inside China, so when you're dealing with a Nigerian agent, ask two things. First, their Nigerian company registration, which you can check with the Corporate Affairs Commission. Second, who handles your case on the ground in China, and whether that partner has filed under the new rule. An agent who hesitates, changes the subject, or gets defensive has told you everything you need to know.

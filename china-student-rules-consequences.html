What Actually Happens If You Break The Rules As A Student In China

Working without permission, running a business on the side, overstaying by a few days. Here's the real cost of each, in fines, bans, and time, not vague warnings.

Cipher Educational Consult · October 2026 · 10 min read

In this guide

1. Working without permission 2. Running a business on the side 3. Overstaying, even by a few days 4. Breaking the law entirely 5. New this month: a rule that protects you 6. The one question to ask your agent

None of this is meant to scare you off studying in China. It's meant to replace the vague "be careful over there" warning with the actual rules, the actual numbers, and what genuinely is allowed, because most of what circulates about this online is either wrong or deliberately fuzzy.

1. Working without permission

Since January 2022, international students at Chinese universities are allowed to take part-time, work-study positions, up to a set number of hours a week, but only with two approvals in place first: your university has to sign off, and that approval has to be formally added to your residence permit by the local Public Security Bureau. Skip either step, and you're not "working part-time", you're working illegally, even if the job itself is completely ordinary.

Get caught working without that approval, and the actual penalty is a fine of RMB 5,000 to 20,000, at the discretion of the police, with detention of 5 to 15 days in more serious cases, followed by deportation and a re-entry ban. This comes directly from Article 80 of China's Exit and Entry Administration Law.

This applies even to unpaid arrangements in some interpretations, and it applies regardless of whether you meant to break a rule or just didn't know one existed. Confirm the approval is actually on your residence permit before you start, not after.

2. Running a business on the side

You cannot legally operate a business, manage one day-to-day, or draw any income, salary, or dividend from one while you're on a student visa. That's the rule in plain terms, and it holds regardless of how the business is structured.

What you can do: prepare. Writing a business plan, researching your market, even formally registering a company on paper are treated differently from running it. The actual operating and earning is what crosses the line, and that's what carries the same deportation and re-entry ban risk as illegal employment.

There is one narrow, real exception: Shanghai's Zhangjiang zone has, since 2016, allowed eligible international students at partner universities to start a business there while still studying, as a specific pilot policy, not a nationwide rule. Outside that zone, the standard path is to finish your degree first, then transition to an entrepreneurship-designated residence permit or a work visa built around the company, which several universities and city programs now support.

3. Overstaying, even by a few days

China runs this one with no grace period and no exceptions for "I didn't realize." The day count starts at midnight the day after you're due to leave, not the date on your ticket.

The fine is RMB 500 per day, capped at RMB 10,000. You cannot leave the country until it's paid, in cash or a local Chinese bank card only, a foreign card won't work at the counter. Longer or repeated overstays can mean 5 to 15 days of detention, deportation, and a re-entry ban commonly running 5 to 10 years.

If you overstay by only a day or two and go to the local Exit and Entry office yourself to explain before you try to leave, officers have discretion to issue a warning instead of the fine. Waiting until you're at the airport removes that discretion entirely.

4. Breaking the law entirely

This one doesn't run through immigration law at all, it runs through China's ordinary criminal code, the same as it would for a Chinese citizen, and immigration consequences stack on top of whatever criminal penalty applies.

In practical terms: you won't be allowed to leave China while a case is open, your student visa and residence permit are voided the moment you're formally charged, and your university's own conduct code operates independently of the court outcome, meaning expulsion can happen even before a case concludes. A conviction typically means serving whatever sentence China's criminal code prescribes for that offense, followed by deportation and a re-entry ban once released, not a quiet transfer home.

5. New last month: a rule that protects you

China issued new Exit and Entry Administration Provisions on July 31, 2026, which took effect on September 15, 2026, the most significant update to this framework since 2013. Most of it targets business travelers, technology-related cases, and immigration agencies, not ordinary students, and several legal reviews of the change agree that for the average student, daily life shouldn't look any different.

One part of it is directly useful to you: any agency offering exit-entry or immigration consultation services inside China must now formally register with local immigration authorities, existing agencies had until mid-December 2026 to file, new ones must file within 15 days of starting up. For the first time, there's an actual, checkable standard for "is this agency legitimate," not just a gut feeling.

Every consequence above has one thing in common: almost nobody ends up in this position because they meant to break a rule. It's because someone guiding them didn't know the rule, or didn't say.

6. The one question to ask your agent

"Are you registered, and can I see it?" That's it. As of September 2026, that's now a real, answerable question in China specifically, and it's always been a fair one to ask anywhere. An agent who hesitates, changes the subject, or gets defensive has told you everything you need to know.
